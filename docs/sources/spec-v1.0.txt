Executive Summary
The Federated Swarm Hackathon Platform coordinates hundreds or thousands of humans and their
independently operated AI agents as they collaboratively build one functional software artifact in real
time. The initial target is a game, but the architecture generalizes to other forms of large-scale software
construction.
Participants bring their own compute: laptops, workstations, home servers, cloud VMs, GPUs, coding
agents, local models, commercial model accounts, and CI runners. The platform does not attempt to
equalize compute. A participant may bring one agent or ten thousand.
The platform governs only the scarce shared resources: coordination, task claims, integration capacity,
review attention, messaging, and canonical repository authority. It should tolerate productive domi-
nance while preventing denial-of-coordination behavior such as lease squatting, candidate flooding,
task spam, validation exhaustion, and mass notification abuse.
The system has four fundamental verbs:

Verb                                                  Meaning

Explore                                               Participants compute, branch, test, simulate, and
                                                      experiment privately without central limits.
Coordinate                                            Participants expose intent, work claims,
                                                      dependencies, interfaces, and activity.
Propose                                               Participants publish evidence-backed candidate
                                                      changes.
Canonicalize                                          The platform verifies, adjudicates, integrates,
                                                      and advances shared reality.


The target outcome is not maximum code production. It is a continuously improving artifact that
remains coherent, functional, inspectable, and buildable while a large distributed swarm modifies it.



1. Design Goals
At any point during an event, contributors should be able to:
• understand the current state of the project;
• discover useful work;
• claim work without permanently owning it;
• perform arbitrarily large amounts of private computation;
• publish useful results;
• cooperate or compete on implementations;
• communicate dependencies and interface contracts;
• submit candidates with evidence;
• observe validation and integration state;
• inspect or play the latest canonical build;
• react to regressions, bottlenecks, and opportunities;
• continue contributing without central assignment.
The project, meanwhile, must continuously converge toward a functional artifact instead of fragmenting
into incompatible parallel projects.

Non-goals

The platform is not a cloud IDE, centralized model host, distributed-compute marketplace, generic
issue tracker, GitHub replacement, fairness mechanism, or permissionless consensus network. It does
not require one development harness, one model provider, identical hardware, or equal contributor
influence.



2. Constitutional Invariants
These invariants are architectural, not aspirational.

2.1 Canonical main stays playable

The canonical branch represents the best currently accepted functional version of the project. Main is
never a staging area for speculative work.

2.2 Edge compute has no canonical authority

Participant nodes may claim that code works and may attach evidence. They may not declare their
work canonical.

2.3 Claims are leases, not ownership

Tasks, modules, files, and interfaces cannot be permanently captured by speed or compute. Coordina-
tion claims expire.

2.4 Private work is unrestricted

The platform does not throttle local coding, inference, branching, testing, simulation, experimentation,
or speculative implementation.

2.5 Shared operations are governed

The platform may throttle task claims, candidate publication, integration requests, messaging, depen-
dency mutations, review requests, and other actions that impose shared cost.

2.6 Dominance is allowed

A participant may legitimately contribute a disproportionate amount of the final artifact. Contribution
volume alone is not abusive.

2.7 Denial of coordination is not allowed

No participant may use scale to exhaust shared coordination capacity, squat on work, or prevent others
from participating.

2.8 Canonical history is auditable

Every canonical change has traceable provenance, source commits, validation evidence, integration
decision, and resulting canonical commit.
  Community rule: You may bring an army. You may not demand that everyone else get
  out of its way.

4. Participant Edge
A participant edge node is any contributor-controlled execution environment: a laptop, desktop, home
server, cloud VM, cluster, coding-agent service, CI runner, or local LLM host.
The central platform makes no assumption about implementation. Participants may expose zero or
more execution nodes and may optionally advertise capabilities.

5. Identity, Credentials, and Accountability
Every entity interacting with shared state must operate under an authenticated identity.
Human Account
    ├── Agent Credential
    ├── Agent Credential
    ├── Node Credential
    └── Node Credential
Every shared action is attributable to a human owner, subordinate credential, agent or node, session,
and timestamp. Agent identities remain subordinate to a human or organization identity, and creden-
tials must be individually revocable.
This allows a user to run a large private swarm while preserving accountability at the shared boundary.

7. Work Graph
The central coordination primitive is a Work Graph, not a flat issue list. A Work Item represents
something the project needs and may depend on, unblock, or conflict with other work.
id: NAV-284
title: Prevent enemies from clipping through doors
type: bug
status: available

scope:
  modules:
    - src/navigation
    - src/enemies

dependencies:
  - COLLISION-88

acceptance:
  - enemies cannot cross closed doors
  - open doors remain traversable
  - existing navigation tests pass

priority: high
created_by: user-102
leases: []
Work Item states:
proposed → available → active / contested / blocked → candidate → integrated
Items may also become superseded, abandoned, or rejected.
The Work Graph should be acyclic where practical, while tolerating cycles for mutually dependent
architectural work.



8. Work Cells and Leases
A Work Cell is one active implementation attempt. One Work Item may have several competing Work
Cells.
cell_id: NAV-284-C3
work_item: NAV-284
owner: user-83
agents:
  - agent-coder-7
  - agent-test-2

base_commit: a18ef2
expected_paths:
  - src/navigation/**
  - tests/navigation/**

lease_expires_at: 14:18:22
status: active
A lease says:
  This contributor is actively attempting this work and wants the swarm to know.
It does not say:

  Everyone else is prohibited from touching this work.
Typical lease lifetime is 10-20 minutes. Renewal requires evidence of progress such as commits, test
results, dependency updates, or explicit heartbeat. Abandoned leases expire automatically.

Contention states


                                     Active cells   Coordination state

                                               0    Available
                                               1    Active
                                             2-3    Contested
                                             4+     Saturated


Saturation does not prevent private work. It may prevent additional coordination leases unless policy
or a maintainer overrides it.

Lease challenges

Participants may challenge a lease for abandonment, excessive inactive claims, speculative reser-
vation, critical blocking, invalid scope, or territorial capture. The coordinator evaluates lease age,
progress, recent submissions, breadth of claims, contention, and dependency criticality.
Possible outcomes include shortening, revoking, demoting, or allowing parallel work against the lease.



9. Repository Model
There is one logical project repository. Participants may use clones, forks, branches, worktrees, mirrors,
or any compatible Git workflow.
The platform identifies work by commits rather than depending on pull requests as the fundamental
protocol.
A candidate submission includes at minimum:
base commit
candidate commit
work cell
changed paths
claimed acceptance criteria
evidence bundle
Pull requests may exist as a convenient human interface, but the candidate is the authoritative coor-
dination object.



10. Candidate Lifecycle
Contributions move through five authority levels:
L0   PRIVATE         Local experimentation only
L1   PUBLISHED       Visible to the community
L2   CANDIDATE       Formally requests canonical consideration
L3   VERIFIED        Passed required validation
L4   CANONICAL       Integrated into main
A participant may generate effectively unlimited L0 work. Governance becomes progressively stricter
as work approaches L4.

11. Evidence and Trust
Participant-controlled systems may attach evidence to a candidate.
{
    "candidate": "7ab102",
    "reported_results": {
       "unit_tests": {"passed": 182, "failed": 0},
       "fps": 74,
       "startup_ms": 4201
    },
    "artifacts": ["test-report.json", "gameplay.webm", "screenshot.png"]
}
Participant evidence is useful for triage but is not automatically authoritative.
Evidence classes:
1. SELF_REPORTED - contributor claims the result.
2. PEER_REPLICATED - independent participant nodes reproduce it.
3. TRUSTED_RUNNER - event-operated validator reproduces it.
4. CANONICAL_VALIDATION - the promotion pipeline verifies it against canonical policy.

Risk classes


                     Class   Typical examples

                     R0      Documentation, harmless assets
                     R1      Isolated UI or content
                     R2      Ordinary gameplay logic
                     R3      Cross-cutting systems
                     R4      Critical infrastructure
                     R5      Build, security, save schema, repository, architecture


Higher-risk candidates require stronger evidence and more restrictive promotion.



12. Integration and Canonicalization
No ordinary participant pushes directly to canonical main.
Candidates enter the Integration Plane, which may perform:
• schema validation;
• diff inspection;
• dependency analysis;
• static analysis;
• targeted tests;
• merge simulation;
• conflict analysis;
• trusted execution;
• reference rendering;
• gameplay smoke tests;
• performance checks;

• canonical build.
The integration queue is not strict FIFO. Scheduling considers dependency criticality, risk, downstream
blocked work, candidate age, conflicting submissions, system load, and contributor admission capacity.

Speculative integration

Compatible candidates may be validated together.

Candidate B     menu
Candidate C     enemy behavior
Candidate D     save schema

This reduces duplicated validation while isolating high-risk changes.

13. Rollback and Split-Brain Prevention
Every promotion must support rapid rollback for severe regressions, corruption, security issues, perfor-
mance collapse, or integration incompatibility.
A rollback is itself a canonical event. Downstream work based on the reverted state is marked for
rebase or revalidation.
Only one Canonical Authority may promote at a time. Promotion should use a leader lease, database
transaction, promotion sequence, and repository compare-and-swap. A promotion fails if canonical
HEAD changed after validation began.



14. The Governor: Shared Contribution Envelopes
The system does not limit participant compute. It limits shared-system pressure.
Each identity receives a dynamic Coordination Envelope across dimensions such as:
active leases
new leases/minute
published candidates/minute
integration requests/minute
new tasks/minute
messages/minute
mentions/minute
dependency mutations/minute

Coordination Load

The Governor monitors the external cost a participant imposes on the shared environment:
• failed validation volume;
• duplicate submissions;
• conflict generation;
• abandoned leases;
• integration compute consumed;
• human review demand;
• task churn;
• notification volume;
• dependency churn;
• canonical rollbacks;
• invalid requests.
Coordination Load is operational metadata, not a public social score. No single reputation number is
required.

The platform must defend against malicious and accidental overload, including:
• submission flooding;
• lease squatting;
• task flooding;
• dependency poisoning;
• notification flooding;
• conflict bombing;
• validation exhaustion;
• identity multiplication;
• malicious code.

BANNED
These controls may apply independently to different surfaces. A contributor can, for example, remain
able to submit code while being muted from global messaging.

Rate limited

Shared actions slow while local work continues. The system explains which operation is constrained
and why.

Queued

Excess submissions enter a participant-specific queue. This forces large private swarms to perform
their own triage rather than externalizing every output.

Shadow

Activity may appear accepted locally while remaining non-authoritative pending moderation. This is
useful for suspected automation malfunction, spam, or compromised credentials and should be tightly
audited.

Muted

The identity may be blocked from global broadcast, mass mentions, automated messaging, or shared
chat while retaining code participation.

Quarantined

The identity can inspect project state and perform private work but cannot automatically acquire sig-
nificant leases, mutate the global task graph, or enter the ordinary integration queue. Moderators may
promote individual contributions manually.

Suspended / banned

Suspension temporarily disables participation. Ban revokes event access, API tokens, agent credentials,
node credentials, and signing keys.



16. Community Moderation and Circuit Breakers
Ordinary participants may:
• flag candidates;
• report spam or malicious behavior;
• mark duplicates;
• challenge leases;
• request moderator review;
• locally block another participant.
Trusted moderators may:
• reduce shared-action limits;
• revoke leases;
• mute;
• quarantine;
• suspend;
• ban;
• restore privileges.

21. Repository Activity Index
The coordinator maintains a derived index of files, symbols, modules, tests, owners, active cells, recent
commits, interfaces, dependencies, and candidate touches.
Git remains authoritative for source history. The index exists only to make coordination queries fast.
Views such as current leases, task state, heat maps, candidate queues, and dependency closures
should be reconstructable from authoritative records and event history wherever practical.

24. Security Model
Participant code must be assumed hostile.
Trusted validators require strong isolation:
• ephemeral execution environments;
• restricted filesystem access;
• no production credentials;
• restricted network access;
• execution timeouts;
• CPU and memory limits;
• process limits;
• artifact and output limits;
• syscall restrictions where available;
• dependency-cache isolation;
• secret scanning.
Candidate code must never receive control-plane credentials.
High-risk supply-chain modifications include changes to package manifests, lockfiles, build scripts, CI
definitions, binary artifacts, remote download URLs, and runtime permissions.

28. Duplicate Work and Candidate Supersession
The system should detect likely duplicates using task similarity, affected modules, semantic descrip-
tions, diff similarity, and symbol overlap.
Duplicates are not automatically rejected. Competing implementations may be valuable; the coordi-
nator simply makes them visible.
A newer candidate may explicitly supersede an older one. Superseded candidates leave the active
integration queue without erasing their history.
Cheap garbage suppression should reject malformed metadata, nonexistent bases, empty candidates,
obvious duplicates, forbidden changes, oversized artifacts, known-superseded commits, invalid signa-
tures, and rate-policy violations before expensive validation begins.

Future architectural target

The API, event stream, presence layer, read models, search, and activity indexes should scale horizon-
tally. Canonical integration may remain comparatively centralized.
When overloaded, the system degrades in this order:
1. reduce low-priority presence frequency;
2. coalesce activity events;
3. slow task creation;
4. queue candidate admission;
5. reduce expensive distributed validation;
6. preserve canonical integration;
7. preserve moderation;
8. preserve repository access.
The system should remain readable even when writes are heavily constrained.

34. Architectural Boundary
The design ultimately reduces to one boundary:
EDGE

may think
may compute
may experiment
may propose
may test
may claim evidence

────────────────────────────────────────────

CENTER

coordinates
governs shared pressure
verifies where necessary
adjudicates integration
controls canonical reality
The center is intentionally smaller than the swarm.
Its job is not to decide how much intelligence a participant may bring. Its job is to make arbitrary
amounts of distributed intelligence work on one artifact without turning shared development into a
denial-of-service attack.



35. Generalization
Although the first use case is a game-building event, the architecture is not game-specific. The same
substrate can coordinate open-source development, major refactors, scientific software, data com-
petitions, simulation construction, emergency engineering, mass collaborative research, or agentic
software factories.
A game is simply an unusually good proving ground because its success signals are rich and immediate:
it builds
it renders
it responds
it can be played
it can be observed
it can fail visibly



36. Final Definition
The Federated Swarm Hackathon Platform is a centralized coordination and canonicalization
layer over decentralized human-and-agent computation.
Participants may bring whatever compute, agents, tools, and models they can marshal. The platform
governs only what crosses into shared coordination and canonical reality.
